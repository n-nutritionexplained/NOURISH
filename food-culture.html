THE MIND · INVESTIGATION

Food culture:
why does a meal
mean more
than a meal?

Nobody cries over a really good spreadsheet.

Show someone their grandmother's recipe, and watch what happens.

01 A SMELL, A DECADE

It happens in the space of a single breath.

A particular spice, a specific bread, the exact way someone's kitchen smells at a certain time of year — and suddenly you're not standing where you're standing. You're eight years old, or eighteen, in a room that might not even exist anymore, with people who might not either.

No other everyday object does this quite like food does. Nobody gets transported back to childhood by a stapler.

So why does food,
specifically, carry
this much weight?

Let's start with the wiring.

02 START HERE

The smell that
skips a step.

Every other sense you have — sight, sound, touch — routes its signal through a brain structure called the thalamus before it reaches anywhere emotional. Smell doesn't. It goes almost directly to the amygdala and hippocampus, the brain's centres for emotion and memory.

Taste works closely with smell for exactly this reason — most of what you experience as "flavour" is actually your nose, not your tongue, doing the work.

Food doesn't just remind you of a memory. It has a shortcut straight to one.

Researchers have tested this directly: in one well-known study, memories triggered by smell were rated as more emotional and more vivid than memories triggered by a photograph of the exact same event — even though the smell-triggered version was sometimes slightly less accurate on the actual details.

Named, appropriately, after the writer who first described it in exhausting, beautiful detail over a biscuit dipped in tea: the Proust phenomenon.

THE SHORTCUT, VISUALISED

Why a smell hits before you've even decided to remember anything.

Smell → Olfactory bulb → Skips the thalamus → Amygdala + hippocampus → Vivid emotional memory

Every other sense waits in line. Smell walks straight to the front — which is exactly why it can ambush you in a supermarket aisle with no warning at all.

EVERY MILESTONE, FED

We don't just eat
at the big moments.
We eat because of them.

Look across virtually any culture, in virtually any era, and the pattern holds: the moments we consider most significant are the ones we insist on marking with a specific food.

Birth

Foods to celebrate a new arrival, or to feed a new parent, appear across cultures worldwide.

Coming of age

From birthday cake to coming-of-age feasts, this transition rarely passes without a specific meal attached to it.

Marriage

Wedding cake, wedding banquets, specific dishes served only at weddings — few life events are more thoroughly catered.

Grief

Mourning meals, wakes, and foods brought to grieving families exist in one form or another almost everywhere.

The calendar itself

Harvest festivals, new year meals, religious fasts and feasts — entire calendars are built around when, and what, to eat.

A species that only needed food for energy would never have invented the wedding cake.

THE COST OF EATING ALONE

Ten out of
twenty-one.

10/21

the average number of weekly evening meals a UK adult eats alone

Out of 21 possible evening meals a week, according to a national survey conducted for Oxford researcher Robin Dunbar's 2017 study on social eating.

That same study found something more interesting than the number itself: people who eat socially more often report being happier, more trusting of others, more engaged with their community, and having more friends they can rely on for support — and when researchers checked the direction of that relationship statistically, the evidence pointed from eating together to feeling closer, not the other way around.

So the useful question probably isn't:

"Is eating together just a nice tradition?"

It's:

"Am I getting enough of something that measurably makes people happier?"
03 YOU ARE WHAT YOU EAT (SORT OF)

Food doesn't just
feed you.
It identifies you.

A French sociologist studying food and identity described eating as an act of "incorporation" — literally taking something outside yourself and making it part of you. His argument was that this isn't just biology. Symbolically, we absorb some of the qualities of what — and who — we eat with.

Choosing what to eat is rarely just a nutrition decision. It's also a quiet vote for who you are.

That's why food fights above its weight in family arguments, cultural pride, and even politics. Refusing a dish can read as refusing a whole heritage. Learning to cook a grandparent's recipe can feel like reclaiming an identity, not just a technique.

And it's why leaving a food culture behind — through migration, illness, or simply growing up somewhere else — can carry a specific, hard-to-name kind of grief. It's not just that the dish is unavailable. It's that a small, repeated way of being yourself has gone quiet.

THE NOSTALGIA LOOP

Comfort food isn't really about the food.

Researchers who study food-triggered nostalgia have found something worth sitting with: the foods people find most comforting aren't necessarily the tastiest, by any objective measure. They're the ones most heavily loaded with memory.

A 2023 study found that nostalgic foods act as "social surrogates" — stand-ins for the people you shared them with, not just flavours you enjoy.

In that research, eating a personally nostalgic food increased people's sense of social connectedness, self-esteem, and even how meaningful they felt their life was — benefits that a generically "delicious" but unfamiliar food simply didn't produce.

Which means when comfort food actually comforts, it's rarely doing it alone. It's doing it on behalf of everyone who was ever at the table with you.

THREE MYTHS, RETIRED

What gets flattened when we call food "just fuel."

THE MYTH

"Comfort food is just about how it tastes."

THE EVIDENCE

Studies on food-evoked nostalgia find the comfort comes largely from the memories and relationships attached to a food, not its flavour profile on its own — which is why a "better" version of your childhood dish can somehow taste less comforting than the original.

THE MYTH

"Eating alone more often is just a harmless modern habit."

THE EVIDENCE

A large UK study found the relationship runs from shared eating to feeling closer to others — not simply that already-close people happen to eat together. Losing shared meals appears to cost something real, not just something sentimental.

THE MYTH

"Traditional food is just a diet preference, like any other."

THE EVIDENCE

Food-identity researchers describe eating as a symbolic act of "incorporation" — food is one of the main ways cultures transmit identity across generations, which is why losing access to a food culture can trigger genuine grief, not mild inconvenience.

THE QUESTION I ACTUALLY CAME FOR

Okay, but is this
just sentimental?

We've established that food carries memory, marks every major life event, and quietly signals identity. All of that sounds lovely at a dinner party. It also sounds like it could be dismissed as decoration.

Which brings us to the actual, slightly more skeptical question.

Does any of this meaningfully matter, or is it just a nice feeling?

The sceptical take sounds fair.
The data disagrees.

The dismissive version of this argument usually goes:

Food's real job is providing energy and nutrients.
Everything else — memory, ritual, identity — is a pleasant bonus.
So none of it is essential to actual wellbeing.
THE OXFORD SOCIAL EATING STUDY

Robin Dunbar's 2017 research didn't just find that people who eat together are happier — using path analysis, it found the causal arrow points from shared eating to increased trust, community engagement and social support, not the reverse.

That's a hard finding to file under "just a nice feeling." It's a measurable wellbeing effect, with a specific behaviour — eating with other people — identified as the likely cause.

The question isn't really sentimental or essential. It's which parts of "food as fuel" thinking quietly cost us something we can actually measure.

CONTEXT MATTERS

So when does this deserve more deliberate attention?

01

Moving to a new country or culture, where food can become an anchor to what's familiar

02

Grief, where food traditions often carry weight other things can't

03

Building a new family — blended, chosen, or otherwise — without an inherited set of food rituals

04

Feeling isolated, where a shared meal is a concrete, low-cost intervention worth trying

The less useful question is:

"What should I eat?"

The more useful one is:

"Who am I eating this with, and does that answer need more attention than the menu does?"

SO, WHAT DID I ACTUALLY LEARN?

Probably not
what I expected.

I went looking for a nice way to say "food is more than fuel." What I found was a specific piece of brain wiring that gives food a shortcut to memory no other sense has, a body of research showing shared meals causally improve wellbeing, and a reason grief over lost food traditions is grief over something real, not something silly.

What I take from all of this is much simpler:
01

Comfort food is doing memory work, not just flavour work — treat it accordingly.

02

Eating with people isn't a nice extra. It's one of the more reliable levers for actual wellbeing we have.

03

Losing a food tradition is a real loss, even when it sounds small from the outside.

Not just sentimentality.

Not a lesser, "soft" side of nutrition.

Just as real, and as researchable, as any nutrient on a label.

Food was never only about keeping you alive. It was also about making sure you didn't do it alone.

AND MAYBE THAT'S THE PART I WAS LOOKING FOR

Understanding gives us
better questions to ask.

I don't think the lesson of food culture is that we need to romanticise every meal.

I think it's that the parts of eating we treat as extra — memory, ritual, company — were quietly doing some of the most important work the whole time.

"What should I eat tonight?"

becomes

"Who could I actually eat this with?"

"Is there a food tradition I've quietly let go of, that I'd like back?"

"Am I treating a meal like a memory, or just like an input?"

And if the honest answer points toward eating alone, again, out of habit rather than choice?

Maybe tonight's the night
to set a second plate.

Because food is supposed to exist within our lives — including the people in them, not just the plate in front of us.

I went into this expecting a soft, feel-good article about tradition. I came out with an actual, testable reason to call someone and ask them to dinner.

Nourish yourself.
And the people you're lucky enough to eat with.
