THE MIND · INVESTIGATION

Cravings: who's
actually asking
for the
chocolate?

One minute you're fine. The next, you can't stop thinking about one very specific food.

So who's really asking for it — your body, or something else?

01 THE 11PM QUESTION

It's rarely "I could eat something."

It's "I need those exact crisps," or "only chocolate will fix this," at a very specific volume, usually at a very specific hour.

You're not exactly hungry. An apple wouldn't do it. A plain rice cake definitely wouldn't do it. It has to be that food, and the wanting has a strange, insistent quality that regular hunger doesn't quite have.

Which raises an actual question, underneath all the jokes about willpower.

Is this my body
asking for something?

Or is it something else entirely — a thought that got loud, for reasons that have very little to do with nutrients?

Let's find out what a craving actually is.

02 START HERE

What even
is a craving?

A food craving is an intense, focused desire for a specific food — not food in general.

That specificity is the whole tell. Ordinary hunger is flexible: give a genuinely hungry person almost any reasonable meal and it does the job. A craving is narrower and more stubborn. Only the thing will do.

Hunger asks for
food. Cravings ask
for one food.

Brain-imaging research points to why cravings feel so pushy: the sight, smell or even the mental image of a highly palatable food activates the same dopamine-driven reward circuitry involved in wanting other rewarding things — not because the food is dangerous, but because your brain is very good at learning what reliably feels good.

None of that makes a craving imaginary. It just means it's happening in a different part of the system than plain, run-of-the-mill hunger.

TWO DIFFERENT SIGNALS

Hunger and craving
aren't the same feeling.

Hunger

Builds gradually. Open to almost any reasonable food. Fades once you've eaten enough, regardless of what it was.

VS

Craving

Can appear suddenly. Fixed on one specific food. Often lingers, or comes back quickly, even after you've eaten something else.

That last difference is the giveaway. If eating a full, satisfying meal doesn't make the thought of that one food go away, you're probably not dealing with hunger anymore.

YOU ARE NOT UNUSUAL

Almost everyone
does this.

75%+

of people report experiencing food cravings

Across both men and women, according to survey research on eating behaviour.

Cravings aren't a personal failing or a sign that something has gone wrong with your relationship to food. They're an extremely common, very human experience — one that shows up more in some contexts than others: late at night, under stress, around your period, after a bad night's sleep, or the moment you've told yourself a food is off-limits.

That list is a clue. Almost none of those triggers are about nutrient deficiency.

01

THE EXPERIMENT

What chocolate cravers actually wanted.

In a classic 1994 study, researchers gave people who regularly craved chocolate a way to test what their craving was really for.

Whenever a craving hit, participants were given one of several things: real chocolate, white chocolate (which contains none of chocolate's active compounds), tasteless capsules filled with cocoa powder (all of the "pharmacology," none of the taste), or a placebo capsule.

The cocoa capsules did almost nothing. White chocolate helped a lot more.

If the craving were really the body demanding a specific chemical inside chocolate, the capsules — containing that chemical, minus the taste — should have satisfied it. They didn't. What satisfied the craving was the sensory experience: the taste, texture and aroma.

The craving was never really about a nutrient. It was about chocolate.

TWO MYTHS, RETIRED

The explanations that
sound right and mostly aren't.

THE MYTH

"My body craves it because it's missing that nutrient."

THE EVIDENCE

It's an appealing idea — your body as a wise machine, sending precise chemical requests. But research on specific cravings, like the chocolate experiment, keeps landing on the same answer: the craving is satisfied by the sensory experience of the food, not by isolating whatever nutrient it supposedly contains.

Cravings are shaped far more by learned pleasure, mood and habit than by a hidden nutritional gap.

THE MYTH

"If I just had more willpower, I wouldn't crave anything."

THE EVIDENCE

Sleep-deprived brains show reduced activity in the regions that make careful decisions, and increased activity in reward regions — high-calorie food becomes measurably more desirable after a bad night's sleep, before willpower even enters the picture.

Stress works on the same system: in laboratory studies, people whose bodies release more cortisol under stress go on to eat more, specifically more sweet food, afterwards.

THE QUESTION I ACTUALLY CAME FOR

Okay, but should I
just fight it?

We've established that cravings are common, mostly aren't your body diagnosing a deficiency, and get stronger under stress and bad sleep.

Which brings us to the question most people actually want answered.

If I get a craving, should I just white-knuckle through it?

The logic sounds good.
Your brain disagrees.

The instinct to just refuse a craving outright makes intuitive sense:

The craving is a temptation.
Giving in would be weak.
So the disciplined response is to say no and think about something else.

There's a well-documented problem with "just don't think about it."

THE IRONIC PROCESS EFFECT

In a line of research going back to the 1980s, psychologists found that deliberately trying not to think about something reliably makes it come back stronger and more often — nicknamed the "white bear" effect. Studies applying this directly to food found the same pattern: people told to suppress thoughts of a craved food showed signs of a stronger, not weaker, pull toward it.

This is also the mechanism behind the "forbidden food" effect. The moment a food gets mentally filed under absolutely not, your brain tends to return to it more, not less — which is part of why strict, all-or-nothing food rules so often collapse.

The question isn't really fight or give in. It's white-knuckling it, or actually addressing what's driving it.

CONTEXT MATTERS

So when is a craving worth a closer look?

01

You've been sleeping badly for a stretch

02

You're going through a genuinely stressful period

03

You've recently cut out a food or a whole category "completely"

04

Cravings feel distressing, constant, or tied to guilt and secrecy — worth talking to a professional

05

You're pregnant, where taste and appetite changes are common and usually not about deficiency

The less useful question is:

"How do I make this craving stop?"

The more useful one is:

"What's actually going on around me right now — sleep, stress, restriction — that might be turning up the volume?"

Address that, and the craving itself often gets a lot quieter on its own.

SO, WHAT DID I ACTUALLY LEARN?

A craving is a message,
not an order.

I went looking for the secret nutrient my body was supposedly begging me for.

What I found instead was a very normal brain process — one shaped by sleep, stress, habit and, ironically, by how hard I'd been trying not to think about the food in question.

What I take from all of this is much simpler:
01

A craving is information about your state — sleep, stress, restriction — more than a nutrient alarm.

02

Fighting it with pure willpower tends to make it louder, not quieter.

03

Actually eating the food, in a normal amount, is often the fastest way to make the thought stop looping.

Not a sign of weak character.

Not your body diagnosing a deficiency.

Just a very ordinary, very human brain, doing what brains do.

The craving was never really the enemy. The fight against it usually was.

AND MAYBE THAT'S THE PART I WAS LOOKING FOR

Understanding gives us
better questions to ask.

I don't think the lesson of cravings is that we should get better at ignoring them.

I think it's that a craving is a message worth reading, not a threat that has to be silenced immediately.

"How do I make this craving go away?"

becomes

"What's actually happening in my life right now — sleep, stress, a diet rule?"

"Would eating a normal amount of this food actually resolve it?"

"Am I making this louder by declaring it forbidden?"

And if the honest answer is that the food was never the real problem?

Maybe you can just have
the chocolate, and move on.

Because food is supposed to exist within our lives.

Not become a nightly negotiation with our own brain.

I went into this looking for a trick to make cravings disappear. I came out with something more useful: a reason to stop treating them like a personal failing in the first place.

Nourish yourself.
Don't fight your brain when you could just understand it.
