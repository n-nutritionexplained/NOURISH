THE FOOD · INVESTIGATION

58g

Protein: when did
a nutrient become
a lifestyle?

Somewhere along the way, "how much protein is in that?" became a normal thing to ask about yoghurt.

So I wanted to know what we're actually asking for.

Protein bars. Protein water. Protein-enriched pasta. A yoghurt that proudly announces it has "20g of protein" on the front, in a font bigger than the word "yoghurt."

Somewhere in the last decade, protein went from being one of three macronutrients to being treated like the only one that counts. Hit your number. Get your gains. Don't waste a meal on "empty" food.

Underneath the marketing, I had a much quieter question: what is protein actually doing in my body?

START HERE

Protein has four jobs.
Muscle is only one of them.

A protein is a molecule built from smaller units called amino acids, strung into a specific order and folded into a specific shape. Along with carbohydrate and fat, it's one of the three macronutrients, providing roughly 4 kcal per gram. But energy isn't really its main job — building and running the body is.

01

Structure

Skin, hair, tendons, cartilage and, yes, muscle — all built from protein scaffolding.

02

Enzymes

Nearly every chemical reaction in your body is driven by a protein built to do exactly that job.

03

Defense

Antibodies are proteins, shaped specifically to recognise and neutralise invaders.

04

Messengers

Several hormones are proteins, carrying signals between organs and systems.

So the real question isn't "how much protein." It's "which amino acids, and from where."

WHY QUALITY MATTERS

Not every protein
source is built
the same way.

Nine amino acids are called essential, meaning your body can't make them and has to get them from food. Animal foods — meat, fish, eggs, dairy — contain all nine in amounts your body uses easily, which is why they're often called "complete" proteins.

Individual plant foods usually fall short on one or two. But your body isn't picky about timing — it keeps a rotating pool of amino acids and draws on it across the day. Combine sources like legumes and grains over a day or two, and the gap closes on its own.

ESSENTIAL AMINO ACID PANEL

Per typical protein-containing meal

Leucine The trigger — signals muscle tissue to start rebuilding
Lysine Tissue repair, collagen formation, calcium absorption
Methionine Metabolism and the synthesis of other important molecules
Threonine Structural protein in skin and connective tissue
Tryptophan A building block for serotonin and other signalling molecules

* Animal foods generally supply all nine essential amino acids in useful amounts on their own. Plant foods usually need a partner — a grain with a legume, for instance — to complete the set.

THE OFFICIAL RANGE

0.83 g/kg

The Nordic Nutrition Recommendations' recommended intake for most adults — around 58g a day for a 70kg person.

10–20%

Of total energy intake, with 15% used as a general planning figure. Nordic and Baltic adults already average 15–19%.

1.2–1.5 g/kg

The higher range recommended for older adults, to help protect muscle and physical function.

A wide range most people are already inside — no shaker bottle required.

TWO MYTHS, RETIRED

The claims that outlived
their own evidence.

THE MYTH

"Too much protein will wreck your kidneys."

THE EVIDENCE

The idea started with people who already had kidney disease, whose damaged kidneys genuinely benefited from eating less protein. That finding slowly drifted into a warning for everyone.

A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis of healthy adults found no meaningful difference in kidney function between higher- and normal-protein diets. Higher intake does make kidneys filter a bit more — a normal response, not damage, in people whose kidneys are already healthy.

THE MYTH

"You have 30 minutes after training or the gains are gone."

THE EVIDENCE

The "anabolic window" is real — it's just not 30 minutes wide.

A widely cited 2018 review of post-workout nutrient timing found muscle protein synthesis stays elevated for roughly 24 to 48 hours after resistance training. Eating protein reasonably close to a workout is sensible. Sprinting for a shaker bottle isn't necessary.

THE ONE GROUP THAT GENUINELY NEEDS MORE

Protein across
a lifetime.

20s – 30s

Muscle mass is easy to maintain. The standard 0.83 g/kg range is generally enough.

40s – 50s

Muscle loss quietly begins. Total protein intake and regular strength training both start to matter more than they used to.

65+

The recommended range rises to 1.2–1.5 g/kg, paired with resistance exercise, to help protect strength and independence.

Protein alone isn't the whole story at any age — but it's in this later stretch that the "eat more protein" advice genuinely earns its keep.

THE QUESTION I ACTUALLY CAME FOR

Okay, but do I need
a protein shake?

We've established that protein is a building material, that plant sources work fine in combination, and that most people in this part of the world are already eating enough of it.

Which brings us to the actual, honest question behind the protein-bar aisle.

Do I personally need a supplement to hit my number?

The logic sounds good.
Most people don't need one.

The pitch for protein supplements usually goes something like this:

Protein builds muscle.
More protein must build more muscle.
So a shake on top of my meals must be a good idea.

The first step is true. The second only holds up to a point. The third depends entirely on what your meals already look like.

WHAT THE RESEARCH ACTUALLY SHOWS

Once total daily protein intake is adequate, research on muscle growth generally shows diminishing returns from piling on more — the body can only use so much for tissue-building at once, and the rest is simply used for energy or excreted.

A protein shake is also, at its core, a food. A convenient, pre-measured one — genuinely useful for someone with a specific target, a busy schedule, or a plant-based diet they're still dialling in. But it isn't nutritionally different in kind from a glass of milk, a tin of beans or a couple of eggs.

The question isn't really shake or no shake. It's am I actually short on protein, or just short on a plan?

CONTEXT MATTERS

So when does extra protein actually help?

01

Older adults, to help protect muscle and physical function

02

Serious strength or endurance training with specific recovery goals

03

Recovering from illness, surgery or injury

04

Losing weight, where higher protein can help preserve muscle and support fullness

05

A plant-based diet that needs a bit of planning to cover all the essential amino acids

The less useful question is:

"How do I get more protein?"

The more useful one is:

"Given who I am and what I'm doing, is my current protein intake actually a problem?"

For a lot of people, the honest answer is no.

SO, WHAT DID I ACTUALLY LEARN?

Probably not what
the label promised.

I went looking for a reason to buy more protein powder.

What I found instead was a nutrient doing far more than building muscle, a myth about kidneys that outlived its original evidence, a 30-minute deadline that turned out to be more like 24 hours, and a population — mine — that's mostly already eating enough.

What I take from all of this is much simpler:
01

Spread decent protein sources across your meals, from whatever mix of animal and plant foods you actually eat.

02

Pay closer attention to it during the life stages and situations where it genuinely matters more — ageing, illness, serious training.

03

Don't let a number on a yoghurt lid decide what counts as a good meal.

Not a nutrient to fear running short on.

Not a nutrient you need to maximise either.

Just a building material, doing its job quietly in the background.

Protein isn't a lifestyle. It's just food, doing one of its several jobs.

AND MAYBE THAT'S THE PART I WAS LOOKING FOR

Understanding gives us
better questions to ask.

I don't think the lesson of protein is that we should all be tracking grams more obsessively.

I think it's that the number on the label was never the point — the overall pattern of what we eat was doing the actual work.

"Am I getting enough protein?"

becomes

"Does my usual way of eating already cover it?"

"Is there a specific reason — age, training, recovery — to pay it more attention right now?"

"Am I reaching for a supplement to solve a problem I actually have?"

And if the answer to those is basically no?

Maybe the yoghurt can just
go back to being yoghurt.

Because nutrition is supposed to exist within our lives.

Not become another number we're quietly failing at.

I went into this expecting a case for eating more protein. I came out with a much smaller, much calmer list of times that's actually true — and permission to stop reading yoghurt lids quite so carefully.

Nourish yourself.
Don't outsource that job to a label.
