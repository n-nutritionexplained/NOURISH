THE BODY · INVESTIGATION 03

Why do we
get hungry?

It's not just an empty stomach.

It's a hormone conversation happening without your permission.

01 THE GROWL

The stomach-growl story is simple: it's been a while, your stomach's empty, it lets you know. Done.

Except hunger shows up on schedule even when there's food left in your stomach. It fades sometimes before you've eaten enough to actually need it to. And it can vanish entirely under stress, then come back with force the moment that stress lifts.

None of that fits an empty-tank story. It fits a hormone story much better.

02 START HERE

Hunger has a
command centre.

A region deep in your brain called the hypothalamus constantly receives chemical reports from your gut, your fat tissue and your blood sugar, and decides, essentially continuously, whether to turn hunger up or down.

It's working from two different kinds of information: short-term signals about your last meal, and long-term signals about how much energy you have stored overall.

Hunger was never one signal. It's a running conversation between several hormones and your brain.

THE HORMONE LINE-UP

Meet the messengers actually running the show.

01

Ghrelin

Made mostly in the stomach. Rises before meals — even at your usual mealtime with no food in sight — and falls once you eat. The closest thing to a genuine "hunger hormone."

02

Leptin

Made by fat tissue. Reports your body's longer-term energy reserves to the brain — the main long-term "we have enough stored" signal.

03

GLP-1

Released by the gut after eating. Slows digestion and signals fullness — and it's the exact hormone that weight-loss drugs like semaglutide are designed to mimic.

04

PYY & CCK

Released from the gut during and after a meal, contributing to the feeling of "I've had enough" that arrives partway through eating.

01

THE PROOF IN THE PRESCRIPTION

Ozempic didn't invent a new hormone. It borrowed one.

Drugs like semaglutide (sold as Ozempic and Wegovy) work by mimicking GLP-1 — one of your body's own natural fullness signals — and keeping it active far longer than your gut normally would.

The drug isn't overriding hunger with something foreign. It's turning up a volume your body already had a dial for.

Which is a fairly direct piece of evidence that hunger really is hormonal, not just willpower — strong enough evidence that it's now one of the most widely prescribed classes of medication in the world.

THREE MYTHS, RETIRED

What the "just willpower" story leaves out.

THE MYTH

"Feeling hungry means something's gone wrong."

THE EVIDENCE

Hunger is a normal, healthy homeostatic signal — it's supposed to show up. Its presence isn't evidence of a problem any more than feeling tired at night is.

THE MYTH

"Thirst is usually mistaken for hunger — drink water first."

THE EVIDENCE

This popular claim has surprisingly little direct evidence behind it. Thirst and hunger are regulated by different, largely separate systems — worth trying if you're genuinely unsure, but not a reliable rule for most people.

THE MYTH

"You should never let yourself feel hungry."

THE EVIDENCE

Mild hunger between meals is ordinary and expected — the hormone system is built to rise and fall throughout the day. It's a signal to respond to, not a sensation to eliminate entirely.

THE QUESTION I ACTUALLY CAME FOR

Okay, but am I
actually hungry?

So hunger runs on real hormones, with a real command centre, and even a real prescription drug proving the system exists. Which brings us to the question people actually want answered at 4pm.

Is this hunger, or is it something else wearing hunger's coat?

The instinct is to override it.
That's not usually the fix.

The common response to feeling hungry at an inconvenient time goes:

I shouldn't be hungry right now.
So this probably isn't "real" hunger.
The right move is to ignore it or suppress it.
WHAT THE HORMONE SYSTEM ACTUALLY SUGGESTS

Ghrelin genuinely does rise around habitual mealtimes, independent of your actual energy needs in that moment — your body partly runs hunger on a learned schedule, not just a live energy reading. That doesn't make it fake. It makes it a system responding exactly as designed.

The question isn't really real hunger or fake hunger. It's responding to the signal, or spending energy arguing with a system that's just doing its job.

CONTEXT MATTERS

So when does hunger deserve a closer look?

01

Hunger that feels constant and unrelenting, regardless of how much you eat

02

A sudden, unexplained loss of hunger over several days

03

Hunger signals that changed noticeably after starting a new medication

04

A pattern that feels genuinely outside your control, worth discussing with a doctor rather than managing alone

The less useful question is:

"How do I make myself stop feeling hungry?"

The more useful one is:

"Is this an ordinary signal doing its job, or a pattern that's genuinely changed?"

SO, WHAT DID I ACTUALLY LEARN?

Probably not
what I expected.

I went in expecting a stomach story. What I found was a small committee of hormones, a brain region running constant calculations, and a class of modern medication that only works because that system is exactly as real as it sounds.

What I take from all of this is much simpler:
01

Hunger is hormonal, not a character test.

02

It can rise on schedule, independent of actual need — that's the system working, not malfunctioning.

03

A genuinely changed pattern is worth a doctor's attention. Ordinary daily hunger isn't a problem to solve.

Not a weakness to override.

Not proof you're eating "wrong."

Just a hormone system, doing exactly the job it evolved to do.

Hunger was never really working against you. It was working exactly as designed.

AND MAYBE THAT'S THE PART I WAS LOOKING FOR

Understanding gives us
better questions to ask.

I don't think the lesson of hunger is that we need to interrogate every rumble before responding to it.

I think it's that hunger was never a failure of discipline — it was a hormone system working exactly the way one would expect it to, whether or not the timing was convenient.

"Why do I keep getting hungry at the 'wrong' time?"

becomes

"Is this my body's normal, learned rhythm showing up on schedule?"

"Has anything genuinely changed, worth mentioning to a doctor?"

"Am I trying to out-argue a hormone instead of just responding to it?"

Because a body is supposed to exist within a life, not spend that life being second-guessed for doing the one thing it's built to do.

I came into this looking for a way to control hunger better. I left with a lot more respect for a system that, it turns out, already knows exactly what it's doing.

Nourish yourself.
Your hunger was never the enemy.
