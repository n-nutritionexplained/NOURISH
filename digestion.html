THE BODY · INVESTIGATION

What happens to
food after
you eat it?

You know it goes in one end and out the other.

The 7-metre journey in between is the interesting part.

01 THE JOURNEY NOBODY MAPS

Most of us can describe digestion in one sentence: food goes in, nutrients get absorbed, the rest comes out. True, technically, in the same way "you drive somewhere and arrive" describes a road trip.

In between, there's an entire tube — mouth to exit, roughly seven metres of it in an adult — doing a rotating shift of mechanical crushing, acid baths, enzyme cocktails, and one of the most absurdly efficient absorption systems in biology.

Let's actually walk the route.

STOP BY STOP

The digestive tract,
one organ at a time.

Mouth

Chewing breaks food apart mechanically, while an enzyme in saliva starts breaking down starch before you've even swallowed.

Oesophagus

A muscular tube that squeezes food toward the stomach using coordinated waves of contraction — it works even if you're upside down.

Stomach

Acid strong enough to dissolve metal (diluted and contained by a thick mucus lining) plus an enzyme called pepsin start breaking down protein.

Small intestine

The real workhorse: bile from the liver emulsifies fat, pancreatic enzymes finish breaking down carbs, fats and proteins, and nearly all nutrient absorption happens here.

Large intestine

Water and electrolytes get reabsorbed, resident bacteria ferment leftover fibre, and what's left becomes stool.

01

A FACT THAT NEEDED CORRECTING

Your small intestine is not the size of a tennis court.

You've probably heard this one: your small intestine, unfolded, has the absorptive surface area of a tennis court — somewhere around 250 to 400 square metres. It's in dozens of physiology textbooks.

A 2014 re-measurement found the real number is closer to 30 square metres — about the size of a badminton court, not a tennis one.

The original estimates assumed the intestine was fully stretched flat, ignoring the fact that living tissue doesn't sit that way. When researchers actually measured post-mortem tissue in its natural, folded state, the number dropped by a factor of roughly ten.

Still genuinely impressive for something folded into your abdomen. Just not court-sized.

HOW LONG THE WHOLE TRIP TAKES

Longer than
you'd guess.

24–72h

the typical total transit time from mouth to exit

It varies enormously between individuals, and with fibre intake, hydration, activity and plenty else — there's no single "normal" number.

Most of that time is spent in the large intestine, not because anything is stuck, but because that's where slow, unhurried fermentation and water reabsorption happen — a process that benefits from taking its time, not rushing.

02 MORE THAN A TUBE

Your gut runs its
own nervous system.

The gut wall contains its own extensive network of neurons — sometimes called the enteric nervous system, or informally the "second brain" — capable of coordinating digestion largely independently of your actual brain.

Cut the connection between your gut and your brain, and your gut can still digest a meal. It just does the job on its own.

The vagus nerve keeps the two in close contact regardless, carrying signals both ways — which is part of the physical basis for the well-documented links between gut activity and mood, stress and appetite. (For what actually lives along that route, see our piece on the gut microbiome.)

THREE MYTHS, RETIRED

What digestive folklore gets wrong.

THE MYTH

"Digestion mostly happens in the stomach."

THE EVIDENCE

The stomach mainly breaks food into a liquid slurry and starts protein digestion. The small intestine does the bulk of chemical breakdown and nearly all nutrient absorption — the stomach is more of a prep station than the main event.

THE MYTH

"Drinking water with meals dilutes your stomach acid and hurts digestion."

THE EVIDENCE

Your stomach adjusts acid output to maintain an effective pH regardless of fluid intake. There's no good evidence that drinking water with a meal meaningfully impairs digestion in healthy people.

THE MYTH

"Certain food combinations can't be digested properly together."

THE EVIDENCE

The small intestine routinely handles mixed meals of carbohydrate, fat and protein simultaneously — that's simply how most meals, in most cultures, throughout history have worked. "Food combining" rules aren't supported by digestive physiology.

THE QUESTION I ACTUALLY CAME FOR

Okay, but does
my gut health need help?

So digestion is more distributed, more efficient and more self-governing than most of us picture it. Which raises the obvious next question, especially with an entire industry built around answering it.

Do I need a digestive supplement to help this process along?

The logic sounds good.
It's rarely necessary.

The pitch behind digestive enzyme and "gut support" supplements usually runs:

Digestion relies on enzymes.
More enzymes should mean better digestion.
So a supplement full of them must help.
WHERE THE EVIDENCE ACTUALLY SUPPORTS THIS

Enzyme supplementation has real, specific uses — lactase for lactose intolerance, or prescription pancreatic enzymes for people whose pancreas genuinely can't produce enough of its own. Outside those specific, diagnosed situations, a healthy digestive system already produces what it needs.

Which means the honest question isn't whether enzymes matter — they clearly do. It's whether you have a specific, identified reason to think yours are insufficient, or whether the marketing is answering a question you never actually had.

The question isn't really enzymes or no enzymes. It's a diagnosed deficiency, or a healthy system being sold a solution to a problem it doesn't have.

CONTEXT MATTERS

So when do digestive symptoms deserve real attention?

01

Blood in stool, or persistent, unexplained changes in bowel habits

02

Ongoing pain, not just occasional discomfort

03

Unintended weight loss alongside digestive symptoms

04

Symptoms that reliably follow specific foods (a possible intolerance or allergy worth testing properly, not guessing at)

The less useful question is:

"What supplement fixes digestion in general?"

The more useful one is:

"Is this a specific, persistent symptom worth a doctor's actual opinion?"

SO, WHAT DID I ACTUALLY LEARN?

Your gut has been
fine without you.

I went in expecting a fairly boring anatomy lesson. What I found was a system that runs mostly on autopilot, corrects textbook errors that had survived decades unchallenged, and does more of its real work in a folded, unglamorous stretch of intestine than in the stomach everyone assumes is doing the heavy lifting.

What I take from all of this is much simpler:
01

The small intestine, not the stomach, is where the actual work of digestion happens.

02

A healthy digestive system doesn't generally need outside help to do its job.

03

Persistent, specific symptoms deserve a doctor. Vague "gut health" anxiety usually doesn't need a supplement.

Not a system that needs constant management.

Not something you're doing wrong by not thinking about it.

Just a quietly excellent piece of engineering, running without your input.

You've been digesting food correctly this whole time, mostly without trying.

AND MAYBE THAT'S THE PART I WAS LOOKING FOR

Understanding gives us
better questions to ask.

I don't think the lesson of digestion is that we need to manage it more closely.

I think it's that the system was built to run without supervision — and most of the products marketed to "support" it are answering a question the body already had covered.

"Is my digestion working properly?"

becomes

"Do I have a specific, persistent symptom, or just a general feeling I should be doing more?"

"Am I eating a reasonable variety of fibre, which is genuinely the thing that helps?"

"Is this a marketing question, or a medical one?"

Because a body is supposed to exist within a life, not become a system that needs a dashboard.

I went into this expecting to come out more anxious about my gut. Instead I came out with a corrected fun fact, a genuine appreciation for the small intestine, and a lot less interest in the supplement aisle.

Nourish yourself.
Your digestive system has this covered.
