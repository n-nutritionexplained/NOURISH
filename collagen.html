THE BODY · INVESTIGATION

Is collagen
worth going
broke for?

Powders. Gummies. Coffee creamers. And a price tag that makes you wonder.

I don't run trials. I read the ones other people ran and tell you what I make of them: what's known so far, who paid for it, and what I think.

01THE ONE-SENTENCE PITCH

It goes like this: your skin is full of collagen. Skin ages. So eat collagen.

It is so tidy that I never questioned it. You eat the thing, you get the thing.

I'm not here to laugh at anyone who bought a tub. The logic is genuinely seductive, and nobody ever teaches you how to check it.

Tidy isn't the same as true. So I went to look.

01

WHAT COLLAGEN ACTUALLY IS

A protein. And your body is full of it.

Collagen is the main structural protein of your connective tissue: what gives skin, tendons and bone their strength.

You'll often read that 25 to 35% of all the protein in your body is collagen. That number traces back to a 1955 estimate, and its author noted that nobody had actually measured it. So: a lot. Exactly how much, we don't know.

Collagen is a protein. And when you eat a protein, your body doesn't file it away as-is.

It takes it apart first. What happens next is the whole question.

THE JOURNEY

Where does it go after the spoon?

Collagen powder → Digested into amino acids and small peptides → Absorbed into your blood → ??? → Your skin

The first steps have been measured. The question marks are what the rest of this page is about.

02WHAT HAPPENS WHEN YOU SWALLOW IT

It's digested.
But not into nothing.

The easy dismissal is “your stomach breaks it down, so it can't do anything.” I believed that too.

In a 2024 study, six healthy adults each took a single 10 gram dose of collagen hydrolysate (a pre-digested powder), and researchers tracked what showed up in their blood. Free amino acids, yes, and also small collagen-derived peptides, mainly one called Pro-Hyp. Between 36 and 47% of the hydroxyproline in their blood, an amino acid collagen is especially rich in, was bound up in peptides.

So the pieces do get absorbed.Absorbed doesn't mean useful.

Two things to flag. There were six of them. And the study was paid for by a company that makes collagen hydrolysate, with several of its employees among the authors.

That doesn't make it wrong. It makes it a first look, not a verdict. And it's the first of several times in this article where the funding line matters.

03THE BIG REVIEWS

Three reviews.
One question.
Not one answer.

The best way to ask “does it work for skin?” is to pool many trials together. I found three recent ones. They did not agree.

2023

26 trials · 1,721 people

Pu and colleagues, in Nutrients. Compared with placebo, hydrolysed collagen improved skin hydration and elasticity. The authors also flagged several biases in the trials they included, and said larger trials are needed.

2025

23 trials · 1,474 people

Myung and Park, in The American Journal of Medicine. Pooled together, trials looked positive. Looking only at higher-quality studies, the benefits disappeared. Their conclusion: no clear evidence that collagen supplements prevent or treat skin aging.

2026

35 trials · 2,534 people

Batool and colleagues, in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology. Hydration improved (standardised effect 0.44), and elasticity too (0.62, after excluding two outlying trials), mostly by week 12. No robust effect on wrinkles. The apparent gain in dermal density didn't hold up. They call for standardised products and follow-ups of at least 24 weeks.

A quick translation of “standardised effect.” On the statistical scale, 0.44 sits between small and medium. It means something measurable showed up on the instruments. It does not mean a different face in the mirror, and I couldn't find a way to translate it into “this many fewer wrinkles.”

So the honest reading is: probably a modest effect on skin hydration, maybe on elasticity, after about 12 weeks. Probably nothing on wrinkles. And that's if you trust the trials underneath. Which brings us to who paid for them.
04WHO PAID FOR THE STUDY

The people selling it
fund the studies.

I want to be careful here, because this is easy to say in a way that sounds like an accusation. It isn't one. Researchers who disclose their funding are doing exactly what they're supposed to do. It's information, not a confession.

But it's information you should have. Here are three studies from this article, and who was behind them:

2024

The absorption study (6 people)

Funded by Rousselot, the company that makes the collagen products tested. Several authors worked for Rousselot.

2018

The bone study (131 women)

Partly funded by GELITA, which makes the collagen peptides used. The authors state the planning, organisation and analysis were done solely by the investigators, and declared no conflicts.

2026

A skin trial (114 women)

Two daily doses against no supplement; both improved measured elasticity and hydration, according to the authors. The peptide maker supplied the product. One author reported consulting fees from the company, and two had fellowships funded by it. The authors say the company had no role in design, analysis or writing. There was also no placebo group.

Now the part that made me stop. The 2025 review above reported that trials without supplement-company funding showed no benefit, while industry-backed trials reported positive effects. An NPR segment that same year quoted a Harvard dermatologist describing it the same way: positive results came mainly from industry-funded studies.

Industry groups pushed back, hard. Between them, three argued that the review misclassified the funding of some trials, that “collagen” covers very different ingredients that shouldn't be lumped together, that industry trials are usually run by independent contract research organisations, and that 17 of the 23 trials were done in Asia at lower doses.

And then the 2026 review said independent trials showed effects comparable to sponsored ones. So who's right? Honestly, it's contested.

What I can say is this: the question “does the effect hold up when the seller isn't the one paying?” is open, and anyone who answers it with total confidence is either very sure of a single review, or selling something.

THREE THINGS I ASSUMED

What I assumed, and what I found.

THE MYTH

“It's destroyed in your stomach, so it can't do anything.”

THE EVIDENCE

It's broken down, yes. But the pieces, including small peptides, show up in your blood after you eat it. Whether they then do anything useful for your skin is a separate question, and it's the one the trials try to answer.

THE MYTH

“Collagen creams put collagen back into your skin.”

THE EVIDENCE

Skin is a barrier. A well-known rule of thumb says a molecule needs to weigh under 500 daltons to get through it. A whole collagen molecule weighs around 300,000. A cream may feel lovely, but it isn't rebuilding collagen.

THE MYTH

“Bone broth is the natural version of a collagen supplement.”

THE EVIDENCE

In one study, broths made to a standard recipe contained clearly less of the key amino acids than a 20 gram dose of the supplements used in collagen research. Broths without a standard recipe varied widely. The authors concluded broth is unlikely to be a reliable source. That doesn't make it a bad food, just not a reliable dose.

05MARINE VS ANIMAL

Does the fish
actually win?

Walk down the aisle and you'll see it: “marine collagen,” often pitched as the better kind. Absorbs faster. Smaller particles. Cleaner. Meanwhile “animal” collagen means cow (hide) or pig (skin), often just labelled “bovine.”

Here is what I found when I went looking for the proof behind that pitch.

THE ONE HEAD-TO-HEAD

Six people, three sources

It's the same 2024 absorption study from earlier. It compared hydrolysates from cow hide, pig skin and fish skin (and two particle sizes of the cow one). Free hydroxyproline in the blood was not significantly different across sources or sizes. Some small peptides did differ: fish and pig products gave higher levels of two of them than the cow one. The authors call it a pilot and say bigger studies are needed. And remember who funded it.

PEPTIDE SIZE

It's about processing, not species

A 2024 lab study on fish skin collagen showed that the peptide size you end up with depends heavily on which enzymes you use to break it down. With a triple-enzyme process, nearly 60% of the peptides were under 500 daltons. That study only looked at fish, and only in cells and test tubes, so it can't say fish beats cow. What it does show is that “smaller” is something a manufacturer can dial in, whatever the animal.

SKIN TRIALS

No answer yet

The 2026 review planned to compare marine and bovine/porcine products, but didn't report a result for either. I did not find a trial that tested marine against bovine for skin outcomes.

THE FINE PRINT

Fish allergy and mystery labels

In a survey of 59 supplements sold near one dermatology practice in Houston, 30 gave no information on where the collagen came from, and 10 listed fish. Nine of those 10 had no allergen warning. Fish collagen can trigger allergic reactions, and hydrolysed fish collagen has been linked to anaphylaxis.

I found no evidence that marine collagen works better than the cow or pig kind. I also found no proof it doesn't. Nobody has done the study.

So the claim is ahead of the evidence. If marine is the one you prefer (you don't eat beef or pork, say), that's a perfectly good reason. “It absorbs better” isn't one I could back up.

06CAN'T YOU JUST EAT IT?

Is it in food?
Do you already
get enough?

Yes, collagen is in food. It's the chewy connective stuff in animals. Cuts rich in connective tissue, like brisket and pot roast, contain it. Bone broth contains gelatin, a form of collagen, though how much varies a lot. Poultry, fish and eggs supply the amino acids your body uses to build its own.

Plants don't contain collagen itself, since it's an animal protein. But beans, tofu and dairy give you amino acids, and fruit and vegetables give you the vitamin C your body needs to make collagen.

Here's what reframes the whole question. Digestion breaks collagen down whether it comes from a pot roast or a scoop of powder. The powder is just that process done in advance. So the real question isn't “is collagen in my food?” It's “am I already getting enough of the building blocks, and does extra help?”

THE MAINSTREAM ANSWER

Eat enough protein, and the nutrients that build collagen

A Mayo Clinic dietitian (Lisa Mejia, 2021) explains that your body makes collagen from amino acids, helped along by vitamin C, zinc, copper and manganese. Her advice centres on enough protein plus those nutrients from food. She notes that supplements are broken down during digestion, so it's unclear whether your body would use them to build collagen where a product says it will, and that few large, long-term trials support recommending them to the general public.

NO TARGET

There's no official collagen requirement

Vitamin C has recommended daily amounts (see below). For collagen itself, I found no recommended intake anywhere. Nothing in the sources I read gives a number you're supposed to hit.

THE CHALLENGE

One group says you can't make enough glycine

Glycine is the most common amino acid in collagen. In 2009, Meléndez-Hevia and colleagues argued that the body's ability to make glycine falls short of what collagen turnover needs. By their calculation, for a 70 kg adult, a shortfall of roughly 8.5 to 10 grams a day that ordinary food (about 1.5 to 3 grams a day of glycine from protein) doesn't close. They call glycine “semi-essential.” A 2018 follow-up from the same group, in cells from three cows, found added glycine increased collagen production.

THE CATCH

A calculation, not a measurement in people

The 2009 paper is a theoretical model. Many of its inputs are extrapolated from rats, pigs and rabbits, and the result depends heavily on how much glycine the body is assumed to recycle. The 2018 study is in cultured cells, not people, and the authors recommend about 10 grams of glycine a day based partly on a study in a different condition. Funnily, by their own numbers collagen powder is a weak glycine source: they put hydrolysate at about 25% glycine, so a 10 gram dose delivers around 2.5 grams.

Do you need to supplement? Nothing I read says you have to. One group argues there's a gap. It's a calculation, not something measured in patients.

If you eat enough protein and vitamin C, the building blocks are there. I did not find a study testing whether people eating normal diets are actually short of them, which is exactly the study that would settle it. I also haven't looked for published rebuttals of the glycine argument, so treat it as an open hypothesis.

THE QUESTION I ACTUALLY CAME FOR

Okay. But is it worth
my money?

This is the question that matters for most people, and it's the one nobody puts on the label.

If the effect is modest, takes about 12 weeks to show, and the evidence is contested, what is a tub of it really buying?

THE PRICE

Do the math
before you buy.

In all my sources, I found exactly one price: about $55 for a bag of collagen powder, in a local news story from 2025. A price per bag tells you nothing, because bags are different sizes. So here is how to work it out:

price of the tub ÷ grams in the tub × 10 = what one 10 gram dose costs you

× 84 = what one study-length stretch costs (about 12 weeks, daily, which is what many of the skin trials ran)

Example with made-up numbers: a €40 tub with 300 g in it. 40 ÷ 300 × 10 = €1.33 a day. × 84 = about €112 for 12 weeks, before you'd know whether it did anything.

The trials I read used anywhere from 2.5 to 15 grams a day, and the 2026 review found no clear link between dose and benefit. Duration mattered more. So if you're paying for the biggest scoop, you're paying for something nobody has shown helps.

And what else could that money do?

01

A Cleveland Clinic dermatologist told reporters in 2025 that the evidence is limited, that she doesn't recommend collagen supplements, and that she tells patients to put their money into proven options: vitamin C and retinoids to stimulate collagen production, and a broad-spectrum sunscreen to protect what they already have. I haven't reviewed the evidence on those myself, so that's her claim, not mine.

02

UV from the sun is the primary environmental factor in human skin aging, according to a highly cited review. That one I can point to.

03

Your body needs vitamin C to make collagen. Without enough, collagen synthesis is impaired, and at the extreme that's scurvy: poor wound healing, joint pain, inflamed gums. The US recommended daily amounts are 90 mg for men, 75 mg for women, 85 mg in pregnancy and 120 mg while breastfeeding. Smokers need 35 mg more. Other countries set their own numbers.

04

A dietitian quoted in the same local story suggested a higher-protein diet (meat, fish, eggs, beans, dairy) with vitamin C, rather than buying the powder. Her point: the building blocks are in ordinary food.

EVERYTHING ELSE PEOPLE TAKE IT FOR

Joints, bones, tendons.
What's actually known.

Skin gets the most attention, but collagen is sold for a lot more. Here's what I could find for each. Tap to open.

Knees and osteoarthritis

A 2023 review pooled four randomised trials (507 patients) on collagen peptides for knee osteoarthritis. Pain scores were lower than with placebo (standardised effect −0.58). The authors rated the evidence for pain as moderate.

But all four trials were rated high risk of bias, the results differed a lot from one trial to the next, and one trial may have driven much of the result. A Boston University osteoarthritis researcher told NPR that many of these studies are industry-funded, that the evidence is unclear, and that large studies are needed. I'd read it as “maybe, and small so far.”

Bone density

One 2018 trial gave 131 postmenopausal women with low bone density either 5 grams of collagen peptides or a placebo, daily for 12 months. Bone density in the spine rose about 3.0% in the collagen group and fell about 1.3% in the placebo group. At the femoral neck it rose about 6.7% versus a fall of about 1.0%.

It's a striking result, and it's one trial. It was partly funded by the manufacturer. About one in five women dropped out (102 finished), though everyone was included in the analysis. I haven't read a second trial confirming it, so I'd call it promising and unconfirmed.

Tendons and ligaments

In 2017, eight healthy men took 15 grams of vitamin C-enriched gelatin an hour before six minutes of skipping, three times a day for three days. A blood marker of collagen synthesis roughly doubled compared with placebo, and engineered ligaments grown in their serum contained more collagen.

It's a clever design and it's eight men. It tells you something happens in the blood. It doesn't tell you tendons got stronger.

Hair and nails

I looked, and I did not find a trial summary I could open and trust. So I'm not going to tell you anything about hair and nails. If you know the literature, tell me.

SAFETY AND WHAT'S IN THE TUB

Is it safe? Mostly. But the label is a mystery.

01

In the knee review, side effects (adverse events) were not significantly more common than with placebo. But the authors rated that evidence very low quality, and the estimate leaned slightly toward more events.

02

In the US, supplements are regulated as food. They don't go through FDA premarket review, and manufacturers set their own standards. Check the rules where you live.

03

The independent testing group ConsumerLab found the toxic metal cadmium in one of 15 collagen powders it tested. The Houston survey authors recommend products with a USP, NSF or ConsumerLab seal, and noted none of the 59 products they surveyed displayed one.

04

If you have a fish allergy, see above. Read the label twice.

MY VERDICT

Would I buy it?
No.

I promised myself a real answer, not a shrug. Here it is, with the reasons attached.

Does it work?

A little, for some things. Maybe.

The most careful reading I can give is: a modest improvement in skin hydration, perhaps elasticity, after about 12 weeks. I don't believe it smooths wrinkles; the newest review found nothing there. Whether the effect survives when the seller isn't the one funding the trial is the open question, and it's the one that stops me saying yes.

Is it worth the money?

Not as a first move.

For the price of a tub, I'd rather put the money into things with a firmer base, according to the dermatologist I quoted and the UV review: sunscreen (UV is the big one for skin aging), enough vitamin C and protein from actual food, not smoking. That order matters. Collagen powder on top of a skipped sunscreen is the wrong way round.

Marine or bovine?

Don't pay extra for “marine.”

I found no proof it works better. Choose on what you eat, what you're allergic to, and price. Not on “absorbs better.”

Do I need to supplement at all?

Nothing I read says you have to.

The collagen in a tub is broken down the same way as the collagen in a pot roast. With enough protein and vitamin C, the building blocks are there. One group argues about a glycine gap, but it's a calculation, not a finding, and not enough for me to buy a tub on.

If someone still wanted to try it?

Match what the studies did.

Pick the cheapest plain product that says what's in it and carries an independent testing seal. The skin studies I read ran around 12 weeks, so that's the window the evidence covers. Decide beforehand what “working” would mean for you, and if you can't tell by the end, don't renew on hope.

What would change my mind: a large trial, run by people with no stake in the product, lasting at least 24 weeks, that measures wrinkles and not just hydration. If that shows up and holds, I'll say so here.

SO, WHAT DID I ACTUALLY LEARN?

Plausible isn't
the same as proven.

I went looking for a verdict. I found a protein with a real job, a supplement whose pieces genuinely reach the blood, and a pile of trials that lean in an encouraging direction without closing the case.

What I'm taking from it:
01

Collagen is your body's main structural protein. You make your own, and vitamin C is part of how.

02

Swallowed collagen is digested, but pieces do reach your blood.

03

Trials point to a modest effect on skin hydration and elasticity, and none on wrinkles. How much of that is real when you strip out company-funded studies is still contested.

04

Collagen is in food, and the building blocks are in ordinary protein foods and vitamin C-rich fruit and vegetables. There's no official collagen requirement, and the “you can't make enough glycine” argument is a hypothesis.

05

Marine isn't proven better than bovine. Nobody has tested it where it counts.

Not a scam by default.

Not a proven fountain of youth.

Not something a cream or a bowl of broth reliably delivers.

The question was never “is it absorbed?” It was “does it do what the label says, and is it worth what it costs?”

AND MAYBE THIS IS THE PART I WAS LOOKING FOR

Absorbed isn't works.
Works isn't
worth it.

I don't think the lesson is that collagen is a scam. The data is more interesting than that.

I think it's that three sentences that sound true, “it's absorbed,” “it works,” and “it's worth it,” are very easy to blur together when someone is selling something.

“Does collagen work?”

becomes

“Compared with what, in whom, and for how long?”

“Who paid for the study, and who sells the product?”

“Is the claim about the ingredient, or about this exact product?”

“What does it cost per week, and what else could that buy?”

Because you don't need a science degree to ask them. You just need to know the question is allowed.

I came in wondering whether I was missing out. I'm leaving with a better idea of what to ask before I spend.

Nourish yourself.
And ask what comes after “absorbed,” and what it costs.
